## 1. The 30,000-ft view

### What's actually true about getting onto US shelves

1. **US buyers don't care about Jamaican sales numbers.** Forty years of heritage gets you a meeting and a story. US sell-through data gets you the shelf. Every buyer asks some form of "how fast does it sell, and where?" The whole first year is about building a US answer.
2. **Retail runs as a ladder, and you can't skip rungs:**
   **Independents & diaspora stores → regional specialty chains → a distributor (KeHE/UNFI or a specialty distributor) → regional programs at Whole Foods/Sprouts → national category reviews → Costco.**
   Costco comes last, and it's a different business (big packs, low margin, huge volume, high risk).
3. **The distributor is a chicken-and-egg problem.** Retailers want you on a distributor they already use. Distributors list you when retailers ask for you. You break the loop with a few written store commitments ("we'll carry it if it's on KeHE"), and by selling direct before that.
4. **Buyers work on a calendar.** Most chains review each category once a year. If you miss the window you wait another year. Learn each target's review dates early and plan backwards from them.
5. **Shelf placement is a strategic choice.** You can sit in the **International/Caribbean set** (heritage buyers, a small set, Grace and Walkerswood already there) or in the **mainstream spice/marinade/hot sauce/grilling set** (bigger, more competitive, and the growth opportunity). Upscale stores like Bi-Rite and Le Beau merchandise by use ("grilling," "pantry") more than by ethnicity, which works in your favor. Decide this per retailer.
6. **Realistic timeline:**
   - Months 1–3: get import-ready, compliant and priced.
   - Months 4–9: 30–75 independent and diaspora doors, a handful of restaurants, first velocity data.
   - Months 10–15: a distributor, a first regional chain, a first Whole Foods or Sprouts regional conversation.
   - Months 16–24: regional chain programs, possibly a Costco regional roadshow, a second and third region.
   - Months 24–36+: national category reviews. **Two to three years to real national distribution is normal, and fast is the exception.** If anyone promises Whole Foods nationally in six months, walk away.
7. **Two engines run at once.** Engine A is **retail** (slow, brand-building, expensive). Engine B is **foodservice/B2B**: restaurants, caterers, prepared-foods departments. B2B is faster, earns cash and credibility, and puts the product in front of eaters. Restaurant demand also pulls you into Sysco or US Foods later.

### Core strategy in one paragraph

Win the **diaspora markets first** (South Florida, NYC metro), where people already know and want the brand, so velocity comes fast. **At the same time** build a **flagship specialty market** in the Bay Area (Bi-Rite, Le Beau, Rainbow, Berkeley Bowl and similar), where you can be on the floor yourself and where the "authentic 40-year heritage" story reaches non-Caribbean food shoppers. Use both sets of numbers to land a distributor, then regional Whole Foods/Sprouts and upscale chains. Costco comes once you can supply it without it sinking you.

## 2. Foundation: what must be true before buyers

Do this in Months 1–3. Buyers will check all of it, and some of it (FDA, labels) is legally required before product can enter the country at all.

### 2.1 Legal & regulatory (import into the US)

| Item | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **FDA Food Facility Registration** | Every facility that makes food for the US must register with FDA. Foreign facilities must also name a **US Agent**. | Registrations renew in **even years, Oct 1–Dec 31**, so **2026 is a renewal year**. If the Jamaica plant is registered, confirm it renews by Dec 31, 2026. |
| **Importer of Record + FSVP** | The US importer must run a **Foreign Supplier Verification Program (FSVP)** under FSMA. | Decide who is importer: your own US entity, or a contracted importer. FSVP needs a qualified person, a hazard analysis and supplier verification records. Consultants do this. |
| **Prior Notice** | Every food shipment has to be notified to FDA before it arrives. | Usually handled by your customs broker. |
| **Acidified foods (if you sell wet jerk sauce, marinade or pepper sauce)** | Shelf-stable acidified foods fall under **21 CFR 108 & 114**. The plant must register (FCE number), file each product's process (SID) with FDA, and have a supervisor trained at a Better Process Control School. | **This is the item most likely to hold up shipments at the border.** Confirm early whether each wet product counts as acidified, using a process authority. Dry rubs and seasonings don't need this. |
| **Spice import scrutiny** | FDA has long-standing import alerts on spices, mainly for *Salmonella*. | Expect sampling and possible detention. A certificate of analysis per lot and a validated microbial reduction step (for dry spices) protect you. |
| **US label compliance** | FDA Nutrition Facts, ingredient list, allergen statement (the **9 major allergens, including sesame**), net quantity in both US and metric units, name and address of manufacturer or distributor, and country of origin. | Get a US label review done professionally. The Jamaican label probably won't pass as-is. |
| **Duty / tariff** | Jamaica is a beneficiary under the **Caribbean Basin Initiative (CBERA)**, so many qualifying goods enter duty-free. | Have a customs broker confirm the HTS code and duty status for each SKU. US tariff policy has changed a lot recently, so **confirm current rates before you price**. |
| **US trademark** | "Island Spice" is a common phrase. Search the USPTO register in **Class 30** (spices, sauces) and file if it's clear. | Do this in Month 1. A trademark conflict found after you're on shelf is a disaster. Use a trademark attorney. |
| **Existing US presence audit** | A 40-year Jamaican brand is probably already in some Caribbean shops in NYC and Miami through an importer. | Find out who brings it in, at what price, and whether there's an exclusivity agreement. Gray-market product at a different price will confuse buyers and undercut you. |

### 2.2 Commercial readiness

- **US entity + bank + insurance.** Retailers and distributors want product liability insurance, commonly **$1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate**, with them named as additional insured. Whole Foods, Costco and the big distributors may require more. Ask for each one's vendor requirements.
- **US warehouse (3PL).** Distributors and chains buy "FOB" from a US warehouse, not from Kingston. A **South Florida 3PL** makes sense: Port Everglades/Miami are short sailings from Kingston, and they sit inside your first diaspora market. Add a West Coast 3PL only when Bay Area volume justifies it. Until then, ship from Florida.
- **GS1 barcodes (UPCs)** on every consumer unit, plus case codes (GTIN-14) on shipping cases. Check whether current Jamaican barcodes are GS1 and scan in US systems.
- **Food safety certification.** A **GFSI-benchmarked** audit (SQF, BRCGS or FSSC 22000) is effectively required for Costco and most large chains and distributors. Independents won't ask. If the plant doesn't have one, start now, because it can take 6–12 months.
- **Ingredient-standard check, before you pitch.** Whole Foods publishes an **unacceptable ingredients list**, and Sprouts and many co-ops have their own standards. Traditional Caribbean seasonings often contain preservatives, MSG, artificial colors or certain caramel colors that may fail them. Check every SKU against each list. You may need a **"natural-channel" version** of 1–2 hero SKUs. This decision drives a lot of the plan.
- **SKU focus.** Launch with **3–4 hero SKUs**, not the whole line. Pick them on (a) US appeal beyond the diaspora, (b) passing natural-channel ingredient rules, (c) shelf life, and (d) margin. A typical set: a dry jerk rub, a wet jerk marinade, a hot pepper sauce and a signature all-purpose seasoning. A foodservice pack (e.g. 1-gallon or 5-lb) of your top SKU makes a 5th.
- **Shelf life and lot coding.** US retailers commonly want **at least 9–12 months of shelf life left** when the product arrives. Print a clear "best by" date and lot code on every unit.

### 2.3 Sales tools

- **Sell sheet** per SKU: UPC, case pack, case dimensions/weight, pallet configuration (Ti/Hi), shelf life, wholesale price, suggested retail price (SRP), certifications, distributor availability.
- **Brand deck** (8–10 slides): heritage, who buys it, why now, US traction, marketing support plan.
- **Retailer marketing support plan**: demos, social, diaspora community events, introductory pricing, shelf talkers. Buyers judge how *you* will drive sell-through, not just the product.
- **Samples**: real retail units, not specials.
- **Wholesale terms**: minimum order, payment terms (expect net 30, and net 60 from some), freight policy (e.g. free freight over $X), and a plan for damaged goods ("spoils").

## 3. Geography

### Why start in these places

The US Census (American Community Survey) shows people of Jamaican ancestry concentrated heavily in **Florida and New York**. Together they hold roughly half the US Jamaican-ancestry population, with meaningful communities in **New Jersey, Georgia, Connecticut, Maryland, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania and Texas**. Pull the current ACS tables yourself for county-level targeting. The broader West Indian population (Trinidad, Guyana, Barbados and others) follows a similar map, and those shoppers buy Jamaican seasonings too.

### Wave 1 (Months 4–12): three beachheads

| Market | Role | Retail targets | B2B targets |
|---|---|---|---|
| **South Florida** (Broward, Miami-Dade, Palm Beach) | Velocity engine. Where the brand already means something. Also your logistics hub. | Caribbean grocers and independents in Lauderhill, Lauderdale Lakes, Miramar, Pembroke Pines, Miami Gardens and North Miami. **Publix** (dominant in FL, with an international/Caribbean set) is a Wave 2 target. Sedano's, Presidente and Bravo independents serve broader Caribbean/Latin shoppers. Upscale: Epicure Market, Milam's Markets. | Jerk spots, Caribbean restaurants and caterers, food trucks. Cruise and hospitality supply (longer cycle). |
| **NYC metro** (Brooklyn, Queens, Bronx, Mount Vernon/Westchester, Newark/East Orange) | Volume plus credibility. NYC buyers are trend-setters for the East Coast. | Caribbean grocers along Flatbush, Church Ave and Nostrand (Brooklyn), Jamaica Ave (Queens), and White Plains Rd (Bronx). Independent chains: Key Food, C-Town, Associated, Food Bazaar (mostly independently owned, so buying is often store-by-store or co-op level). Upscale: Zabar's, Citarella, Westside Market, Eataly, Murray's, plus Union Market and Foragers-type stores. | Caribbean restaurants (huge density), caterers, specialty foodservice distributors. Chef's Warehouse and Baldor are NYC-based and serve chefs. |
| **SF Bay Area** (assumed home base, from your examples) | Flagship specialty market: proves the brand appeals beyond the diaspora. You can demo in person. | **Bi-Rite Market, Le Beau Market**, Gus's Community Market, Rainbow Grocery Co-op, Berkeley Bowl, Market Hall Foods, Mollie Stone's, Draeger's, Piazza's, Woodlands Market, Good Earth (Marin), Oliver's (Sonoma), Nugget Markets (Sacramento region). | Bay Area restaurants doing Caribbean, BBQ or "global grill" food. Corporate cafeterias run by contract caterers (Bon Appétit Management Co. is SF-based and runs many tech cafés). |

### Wave 2 (Months 12–20)

- **Atlanta metro**: large and growing Caribbean community, plus Sprouts and Whole Foods presence and Publix. Upscale: Star Provisions, Savi Provisions.
- **DC / Maryland / Northern Virginia** (Prince George's and Montgomery counties): MOM's Organic Market, Wegmans, Whole Foods mid-Atlantic, Caribbean grocers.
- **Connecticut** (Hartford, Bridgeport/Stamford): a strong Jamaican community. Stew Leonard's, Whole Foods, independents.
- **Boston / Philadelphia**: Formaggio Kitchen, Savenor's (Boston); Caribbean grocers in Mattapan, Dorchester, and West and North Philadelphia.
- **Los Angeles / Orange County**: Erewhon, Gelson's, Bristol Farms, Lazy Acres, Mother's Market. Erewhon is a trend-maker, but its standards and costs are steep.

### Wave 3 (Months 20–36)

- **Texas** (Houston, Dallas, Austin): Central Market (H-E-B's upscale banner) and H-E-B; Whole Foods HQ is in Austin.
- **Chicago**: Mariano's, plus independent specialty stores.
- **Pacific NW**: PCC Community Markets, Metropolitan Market, Town & Country, New Seasons, Market of Choice.
- **Midwest specialty**: Zingerman's (Ann Arbor), Plum Market, Lunds & Byerlys, Kowalski's, Dorothy Lane, Heinen's.
- **Southeast specialty**: The Fresh Market (HQ Greensboro, NC). Orlando and Tampa diaspora.
- **Northeast regional**: Wegmans, Kings Food Markets.

**Rule:** don't open a new region until the last one is **re-ordering by itself**. Spreading thin and unsupported is the classic way new brands die: a store sells through once, nobody demos, nobody re-orders, and the product is delisted.

## 5. How each target actually buys

### Whole Foods Market (Amazon-owned)
- **Distributor:** primarily **UNFI**. Some items go direct-store-delivery or through other distributors.
- **Path:** **regional** first (regional buyers can approve items for their region), then **national**. Imported brands usually go through a **regional or national category buyer**, not the local forager. The local program is generally for products made in or near the region. Whole Foods has also run emerging/local accelerator programs, so check current eligibility.
- **Gatekeepers:** ingredient quality standards (the unacceptable ingredients list), insurance, food-safety documentation.
- **Commercial:** expect free fill, promo participation (e.g. Prime member deals) and demo support. The prepared foods department is a separate opportunity.
- **Timing:** category reviews on a set cycle. Find out your category's.

### Sprouts Farmers Market (HQ Phoenix)
- **Distributor:** **KeHE** is Sprouts' primary distributor; Sprouts also runs its own distribution centers.
- **Path:** category buyer + new-item submission. Sprouts publicly pushes "innovation" and differentiated items. Authentic Caribbean heritage fits, *if* ingredients meet their standards.
- **Footprint:** strong in CA, AZ, TX, CO, and expanding in FL/GA/Southeast and the Mid-Atlantic. That overlaps with your Florida and Atlanta diaspora markets.

### Costco
- **Structure:** **regional buyers** (Southeast, Northeast, Bay Area, Los Angeles, Midwest, Texas, Northwest and so on) plus national buyers. Regional is the realistic entry.
- **Path:** a regional item test, often through a **roadshow** (a temporary demo-driven event run by specialist roadshow companies). If it sells, it can become a regional item and later go national.
- **Requirements:** club pack (multi-pack or large format), GFSI audit, strong insurance, ability to ship full pallets to Costco depots on time, aggressive price per unit. Costco Business Centers carry foodservice sizes, which could suit your gallon pack.
- **Warning:** Costco volume can outrun your supply chain and cash. Don't start until you can fund and produce a big order and absorb a markdown if it doesn't sell through.

### Upscale independents (Bi-Rite, Le Beau and comparable stores)
- **Buying:** a store owner or category/grocery buyer decides, often quickly. They may buy **direct**, through a **specialty distributor** or through **Faire**. Ask each one which.
- **What wins:** story, quality, uniqueness in their set, staff tasting it, a demo, and the brand showing up in person. Bi-Rite in particular is known for curating with intent and caring about producer relationships.
- **What loses:** a commodity look, an ingredient panel that looks "conventional," no follow-up.
- **Comparable stores by city:** see Section 3.

### Caribbean / diaspora grocers
- **Buying:** the owner, often supplied by local Caribbean import-distributors. Price-sensitive, and loyal to the brands they grew up with, which is your advantage.
- **Watch for:** existing gray-market supply of your own product at different prices.

### Conventional chains (later)
- Publix, Kroger/Ralphs, Albertsons/Safeway, ShopRite, Stop & Shop, Walmart: often in the **international/Hispanic/Caribbean set**, and often through ethnic specialty distributors or brokers. Slotting fees are common and can be large. Wave 3+ unless a Publix international buyer bites early in Florida.

## 6. The math (price waterfall)

Do this per SKU, per channel. **Example only.** Plug in your real numbers.

**Example: 10 oz jerk marinade, target shelf price $8.99**

| Step | Typical range | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Suggested retail price | — | $8.99 |
| Retailer margin (specialty/natural) | 35–45% | 40% → retailer pays **$5.39** |
| Distributor margin | 20–30% | 25% → distributor pays you **$4.04** (your FOB price) |
| Broker commission | 3–5% of FOB | 5% → –$0.20 |
| Trade spend (promotions, free fill, chargebacks, spoils) | 10–20%+ of gross | 15% → –$0.61 |
| Freight to distributor + 3PL handling | varies | –$0.35 |
| **Net to you before product cost** | | **≈ $2.88** |
| Landed product cost (product, packaging, ocean freight, brokerage, duty if any, FSVP and testing per unit) | — | must be well under this |

- If landed cost is **$1.75**, you keep about **$1.13 a unit (~39% of net)** through the distributor channel. Workable.
- Selling **direct to independents** at about the retailer's cost ($5.00–5.39) skips the distributor and broker. Much better margin, but you do the delivery, invoicing and collections. That's why Phase 1 is direct.
- **Costco:** a different pack, about 14–15% retailer markup, few or no distributor layers, but demos and roadshows cost money.
- **If the waterfall doesn't work at the distributor rung, fix it now** (pack size, price, cost, SKU) before you're locked into a chain price. Raising price after launch is very hard.

## 7. What to track (from Month 4 onward)

- **Velocity:** units / store / week / SKU. Ask each buyer what the average is in their set. You want to be at or above the middle. Slow-turning categories like spices can survive at low numbers; hot sauce and marinades are judged harder in summer.
- **Re-order rate:** % of stores re-ordering within 60 days.
- **Time to re-order:** days.
- **Demo lift:** units sold on demo days vs normal weeks.
- **Distribution:** doors, by region and channel.
- **Deductions:** every chargeback from a distributor or retailer, reconciled monthly. Unreconciled deductions silently erase margin.
- **Cash:** months of runway at current trade spend.
- **Restaurant accounts:** count, monthly volume, menu mentions.

Keep it in one spreadsheet, updated weekly. This spreadsheet *is* the pitch to the next buyer.

## 8. Restaurants & B2B (Engine B)

Run this alongside retail from Month 5.

1. **Independent Caribbean restaurants and jerk spots** (FL, NYC first). Easiest yes, because they already know the brand. Sell the foodservice pack direct or through the Caribbean import-distributor that already delivers to them.
2. **Non-Caribbean restaurants**: BBQ, "global grill," fast casual, food trucks, Bay Area restaurants. Sell the authenticity: a real 40-year Jamaican recipe vs. a generic "jerk" blend. A menu credit ("Island Spice jerk chicken") is free marketing.
3. **Caterers and event companies**: Caribbean weddings and events in the diaspora; corporate catering in SF.
4. **Grocery prepared foods / hot bars** (Whole Foods, regional chains): a separate buyer, sells volume and reinforces the retail item.
5. **Specialty foodservice distributors**, e.g. Chef's Warehouse (specialty to chefs; NYC-based, now national), Baldor (Northeast). They like chef-requested items.
6. **Broadline distributors**: Sysco, US Foods, Performance Food Group, Gordon Food Service. Gotten into through **restaurant pull**: chefs ask their rep, it starts as a special order, and becomes a stocked item once enough customers order it. Each distributor's local operating company has its own buyers.
7. **Cash & carry**: Restaurant Depot (members-only) and Costco Business Center, where small restaurants shop.
8. **Contract dining** (Month 19+): Aramark, Sodexo, Compass Group (incl. Bon Appétit Management Co.) for universities, hospitals and tech campuses. Slow, but big and sticky once you're in.
9. **Ingredient / private label (optional)**: meal kits, prepared-food makers, or co-manufacturers who want an authentic jerk base. Good margin and volume, low brand visibility. Do it only if it doesn't undercut retail.

Foodservice needs **different packs** (gallon or 5-lb), **different pricing** (per pound or ounce) and a **spec sheet** (ingredients, allergens, yield, shelf life).

## 9. Budget categories (get real quotes)

Rough ranges for planning only. They vary widely.

| Category | Notes |
|---|---|
| Regulatory & compliance setup | FSVP consultant, label review, process authority (if acidified), customs broker setup, trademark filing. Low five figures is common for the full package. |
| GFSI certification | Gap assessment, upgrades, audit. Often ~$10K–30K+ in year one, more if the plant needs real upgrades. |
| Insurance | Product liability. Depends on revenue and coverage. |
| Inventory & landed freight | Your biggest cash need. Chain and Costco orders multiply it. |
| 3PL | Receiving, storage per pallet per month, pick/pack, outbound. |
| Trade shows | Booth, travel, samples, shipping. A 10×10 at Fancy Food plus travel can easily run five figures; JAMPRO/Caribbean Export pavilions can reduce this. |
| Demos | Paid demo agencies charge per demo (often in the low hundreds of dollars per 3–4 hr shift). Budget for many. |
| Trade spend | Free fill, intro promos, chargebacks, spoils. Plan 10–20%+ of gross sales in the first years. |
| Slotting | Rare at independents. Whole Foods and Sprouts mostly use free fill and promos instead of classic slotting. Conventional chains may charge real slotting. |
| Brokers / reps | ~3–5% commission, plus a possible retainer. |
| Marketing | Social, influencer/creator content (Caribbean food creators are a real asset), shelf talkers, community events. |
| Syndicated data | SPINS/Circana/NielsenIQ, from Phase 3, if at all. |

**Funding reality:** brands usually run out of cash in Phase 2–3, when chain inventory and trade spend hit before payments arrive (net 30–60, minus deductions). Line up working capital (inventory financing, purchase-order financing or investors) **before** you need it, ideally by Month 9–12. Look into Jamaican export financing too (e.g. through the EXIM Bank of Jamaica), and US small-business lenders once you have a US entity with history.

## 10. Risks & what to verify

### Biggest risks
1. **Shipments held at the border** over FDA registration, FSVP, acidified-food filings or spice microbiology. *Fix it in Phase 0.*
2. **Ingredients that fail natural-channel standards**, which blocks Whole Foods and Sprouts. *Check in Month 1. Decide on reformulation early.*
3. **Running out of stock** at a new chain: delisting, and the buyer remembers. *Safety stock, realistic forecasts, a hurricane-season buffer.*
4. **Cash crunch** from chain orders plus trade spend plus slow payment. *Line up working capital early.*
5. **Spreading too thin**: too many regions, no demos, no re-orders. *Follow the wave rule.*
6. **Trademark conflict** in the US. *Search and file in Month 1.*
7. **Channel conflict / gray market**: an existing importer selling at different prices. *Audit and settle in Month 1.*
8. **Tariff or trade policy changes** affecting Caribbean imports. *Re-check duty status before each pricing decision.*

### Things that change, so verify before acting
- [ ] Current trade show dates and locations (Fancy Food, Expo West, NRA Show, ECRM sessions, Expo Jamaica).
- [ ] Whole Foods and Sprouts current supplier submission process and category review calendars.
- [ ] Whole Foods unacceptable ingredients list (current version) and Sprouts standards.
- [ ] Costco regional buyer contacts and roadshow companies in each region.
- [ ] Distributor coverage, new-item terms and fees (KeHE, UNFI, specialty and ethnic distributors).
- [ ] FDA rules: registration, FSVP, acidified foods, import alerts, and any FSMA rule compliance dates.
- [ ] HTS code and duty status per SKU (CBERA eligibility, current tariffs).
- [ ] JAMPRO / Caribbean Export programs, funding and deadlines.
- [ ] Faire and other marketplace commission rates.
- [ ] Census ACS data for county-level diaspora targeting.

## One-page cheat sheet

- **Months 1–3:** compliance, labels, pricing, inventory landed in Florida. Sell nothing until done.
- **Months 4–9:** South Florida + NYC (velocity) + the Bay Area (specialty proof). 50–70 doors. Demos every weekend. Restaurants in parallel. Collect commitment letters. Track everything.
- **Month 9 gate:** 60%+ re-order rate? Then move on; if not, stay.
- **Months 10–15:** distributor, first regional chain, Whole Foods/Sprouts regional conversations, Wave 2 (Atlanta, DC/MD). Costco exploration.
- **Month 15 gate:** distributor live + a chain + GFSI + 6 months of cash? Then move on.
- **Months 16–24:** chain launches supported hard, Costco roadshow, exhibit at Summer Fancy Food 2028, foodservice distributors through restaurant pull, Wave 3 planning.
- **Always:** sales data beats heritage stories, re-orders beat new doors, never run out of stock, reconcile every deduction.
